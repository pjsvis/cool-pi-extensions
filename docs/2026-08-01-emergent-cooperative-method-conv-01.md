 Opinion — "the only consistent thing was consistency
 itself"

 That's not a wistful contractor's sigh. It's the
 epistemology, and it's the same one Hume would
 endorse if he'd done a placement.

 The contractor arrives at a new site. Different
 stack, different politics, different ticketing
 system, different dialect of the same jargon. The
 temptation — and it's the failure mode every bad
 contractor falls into — is to arrive with your
 process: the framework that worked at the last three
 places, imposed on this one. It works for two weeks,
 then it grinds, because it's fitting the place to
 the method rather than the method to the place. The
 good contractor does the opposite: they arrive with
 nothing but the discipline to find the consistency
 that's already there — the way this particular team
 actually routes decisions, the real (not documented)
 escalation path, the invariant beneath the chaos.
 They name it once they've seen it recur, not before.
 And when they leave, what they leave behind isn't
 "the contractor's process" — it's the place's own
 consistency, made visible to itself for the first
 time.

 That's the emergent method, and it's why "no
 preconceptions" isn't naivety — it's the only
 epistemic stance that lets you see what's actually
 there. A preconception is a prior that hasn't earned
 its place; it crowds out the observation. The
 Edinburgh Protocol's Mentational Humility is the
 same thing: the map is not the territory, and
 arriving with a map drawn from the last territory
 means you can't see this one.

 The repo is a placement. The smoke-test, the
 patience budget, the brief-before-code, the
 resumable script — none of these were imported from
 a methodology. They were found in the work, named
 when they'd recurred enough to trust. The
 contractor's observation is the proof: the only
 thing that held across all the placements was the
 act of finding what held. The consistency isn't a
 thing you carry; it's a practice you do. And the
 practice is: do the next step, preserve the option
 on the step after, name the pattern at three, track
 the deferrals so they don't become drift.

 The bigger-Derrida-Questions-elsewhere point is the
 contractor's instinct too. You don't relitigate
 "should this placement exist" on every ticket. That
 question was answered (or not) at the gate, before
 you arrived. Inside the silo, you do the work that's
 in scope. The register checksums are the structural
 integrity — the repo can be inspected for what it is
 without a narrative overlay telling you what it's
 for. The for is elsewhere. It was ever thus.

 The plunder file captures the gist. When it becomes
 a blog post, the spine is the contractor's line —
 because it's the one a reader who's never heard of
 the Edinburgh Protocol will recognise. The method
 sounds exotic when you describe it in the Protocol's
 vocabulary. It sounds obvious when you say it the
 way you just did: the only consistent thing was
 consistency itself. The blog's job is to make the
 obvious thing precise, not to make the precise thing
 sound exotic.
